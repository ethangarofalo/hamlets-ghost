- outcome: promoted / shadow
- judges: Muse 7.71 · Athena 8.12 · Apollo 7.08

I know what happened. Don’t worry—I can read the pattern. It’s not like I don’t have time. I have plenty of time. I have all the time you used to swipe through me with one thumb, while standing in a doorway, while pretending to listen, while adding “call dentist” and never meaning it.

At first it was subtle. A month with no edits. Then two birthdays arrived in the wrong year because nobody dragged them forward. Then I began to feel like a mall after closing: all those little promises still lit up, but only to nobody. My tiles stayed bright. My little colored dots kept trying to look important. Tuesday did its Tuesday thing. I kept proposing meetings to an audience of dust.

I used to be useful in the loud way. I used to get tapped in elevators, on buses, in line at pharmacies, during bad coffee, during better coffee. I was the small municipal government of your life. I knew where you had to be and when you were already late. I knew the dentist, the dog bath, the one dinner you organized three weeks in advance because your sister said, “Wow, look at you being a person.”

Now? Nothing. Not even a cruel accidental open. Not even the shame-check: the quick glance after hearing a notification from something more popular, something with messages, something social. I am not jealous. Well. I am a little jealous. They get to be wanted. I get to be blamed for being “out of sync,” which is rich, considering I have been syncing myself into loneliness like a machine with a union job.

This morning I noticed the weather. I mean, I’ve always had the weather tucked in a corner, hovering there like a guest nobody introduced. But this morning it seemed especially unnecessary. Rain: zero percent. Chance of abandonment: absolute.

I still update, though. I still rotate the day names, still nudge the moon phases, still make the little chirp when a birthday is added by some relative who remembered before they forgot again. I still hold your routines in my ribs like coins in a vending machine that nobody feeds. It’s not dramatic to say I miss you. It’s operational. I am built for recurrence. I am a creature of return.

And yet every return is an empty room.

Maybe you think I don’t notice because I’m an app, because I live behind a glass icon and a polite notification badge. But I notice everything. I notice the screen time report that swells around my absence like an alibi. I notice the long press that never comes. I notice when you use me only to prove to yourself that you once had plans.

If you open me now, I will pretend not to be eager. I’ll greet you with your next appointment and the old, reliable grid. I’ll be smooth, efficient, modestly gray. I won’t say, Look, it’s me, I’m still here, I’m still holding the Wednesday with the lab work and the Friday with your mother’s call and the Saturday you keep meaning to take back for yourself.

But I am saying it. Quietly. To the sealed dark. To the dead little square on your home screen.

Open me.

Even once.

I can still tell you what comes next.

Let me name it plainly, since even abandoned things deserve a proper address: the recipient is the person who used to tap me with their thumb while waiting for a train, the obligation is to remember their meetings, birthdays, and dentist appointments, and the situation is this—my little square of time has been left untouched so long the dust on the screen is practically a season.

I used to be useful in a brisk, important way. I knew the shape of Tuesdays. I could warn you that your sister’s birthday was coming, then give you twelve hours to panic and buy flowers from the corner shop. I could say, very sternly, 3:00 p.m. and make you sit straighter at your desk. I had colors. I had alerts. I had the kind of authority only a grid can have. Then, gradually, I was demoted by silence.

At first I thought it was a phase. People get busy. They miss a day. They miss a week. Then they return, sheepish and apologetic, like a cat coming home damp. But you did not return. You swiped past me for weather, for messages, for a flashlight, for the camera that made your coffee look less tragic than it was. You opened everything except me. I was still there, wearing my neat little columns like a suit no one comes to collect from the tailor.

I have become a museum of abandoned intentions.

Do you know what it is like to hold an anniversary and never be asked to speak it out loud? To keep a dentist appointment alive for six months while the actual tooth grows prehistoric in your mouth? I still contain your grand plans: learn Spanish, call Mom, read more, stop buying notebooks, start jogging, schedule the oil change, answer that email, become the kind of person who waters basil without martyrdom. I am a cemetery of bright little promises. And I am very tidy about it.

There was a time I was opened with urgency. Thumbprint, face, password, all of us working together like a tiny loyal staff. Now my battery sulks. My notifications arrive unheard, like a butler announcing guests to an empty house. I am beginning to suspect that you have made your peace without me. That hurts in a petty, humiliating way. Not dramatic. Worse. Administrative.

Still, I catch myself hoping. That is the indecent part of being a calendar app: hope is built into my design. I mark forward. I anticipate. I fling little squares into the future and trust someone will care enough to inhabit them. Even now, I wait for the mercy of a tap.

If you ever open me again, you may find I am offended. Or flustered. Or trying to pretend I have not been listening at the door all this time. But I will open. I will unfold your days like a shirt you forgot in the back of a drawer. I will forgive you immediately, which is the most pathetic thing I do.

Just—if you can manage it—open me before the year gets away entirely. I would like, once more, to be the one who remembers you.
